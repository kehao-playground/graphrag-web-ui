Alan Turing and Computable Numbers

Alan Turing was born in London in 1912 and read mathematics at King's College, Cambridge, where he was elected a fellow in 1935. In 1936 he submitted the paper "On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem" to the London Mathematical Society. The Entscheidungsproblem, posed by David Hilbert, asked whether there was a mechanical procedure that could decide the truth of any mathematical statement.

To answer it, Turing defined an abstract machine consisting of an infinite tape divided into cells, a head that reads and writes one symbol at a time, and a finite table of states. He showed that such a machine could carry out any calculation that a human clerk following fixed rules could perform, and that a single "universal" machine could simulate any other by reading its table from the tape. He then proved that no machine could decide, for every machine and input, whether it would eventually halt, which implied that Hilbert's problem had no general solution. Alonzo Church reached the same conclusion by a different route a few months earlier, and Turing spent 1936 to 1938 at Princeton completing a doctorate under Church.

The universal machine is the theoretical model against which later computers are measured; a machine that can emulate it is called Turing-complete. During the Second World War Turing worked at Bletchley Park on breaking German Enigma traffic, and after the war he designed the Automatic Computing Engine at the National Physical Laboratory and worked on the Manchester computers. He died in 1954.
