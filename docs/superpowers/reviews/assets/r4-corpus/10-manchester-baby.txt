The Manchester Baby

The Small-Scale Experimental Machine, nicknamed the Baby, was built at the University of Manchester by Frederic Williams, Tom Kilburn and Geoff Tootill. It was designed to test the Williams tube, a memory device that stored bits as charged spots on the face of a cathode-ray tube and refreshed them continuously. Williams had developed the tube at the Telecommunications Research Establishment during the war and brought the work to Manchester in 1946.

The Baby ran its first program on 21 June 1948. It had a memory of 32 words of 32 bits, seven instructions, and could execute about 700 instructions per second. The first program, written by Kilburn, found the highest proper factor of 2 to the power 18 by trial subtraction and took 52 minutes to run, executing about 3.5 million instructions. It was the first machine in the world to run a program stored electronically in memory, satisfying the design described in von Neumann's EDVAC report.

The Baby was quickly expanded into the Manchester Mark 1, on which Alan Turing wrote programs after he moved to Manchester in 1948, and which added a magnetic drum for secondary storage and index registers, an innovation the Manchester team called the B-tube. Ferranti built a commercial version, the Ferranti Mark 1, delivered in February 1951, which is usually counted as the first commercially available general-purpose electronic computer.

A working replica of the Baby, completed in 1998 for the fiftieth anniversary, is displayed at the Science and Industry Museum in Manchester.
