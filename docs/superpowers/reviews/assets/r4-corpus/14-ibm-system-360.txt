The IBM System/360

By the early 1960s IBM sold several incompatible computer lines, and customers who outgrew one machine had to rewrite their software to move to the next. In 1961 a task force led by Bob Evans recommended replacing every product with a single family whose members shared one instruction set, so that a program written for the smallest model would run unchanged on the largest. IBM chairman Thomas J. Watson Jr. approved the plan, which cost about five billion dollars, more than IBM's annual revenue, and later called it the biggest gamble the company ever took.

The System/360 was announced on 7 April 1964 with six models spanning a fiftyfold range in performance. Gene Amdahl was the chief architect. The family introduced the 8-bit byte and 32-bit word as standards, byte-addressable memory, and a general-register architecture. Smaller models implemented the instruction set in microcode, a technique Maurice Wilkes had proposed in 1951, which let cheap hardware present the same interface as the fastest machines. The 360 used IBM's Solid Logic Technology hybrid circuits rather than integrated circuits.

The operating system, OS/360, fell badly behind schedule; its manager Fred Brooks drew on the experience for his 1975 book "The Mythical Man-Month", which argued that adding people to a late software project makes it later. Despite the delays, orders far exceeded forecasts, and the System/360 secured IBM's dominance of the mainframe market for a generation. Its instruction set survives in IBM's z/Architecture mainframes today, and 80-column punched cards descended from Hollerith's design remained a common input medium throughout the 360 era.
